      Exercises

      Try each one yourself before opening the answer. Handy rule: $\lambda\,(\mathrm{nm}) \approx \dfrac{1240}{\Delta E\,(\mathrm{eV})}$.

      1. Find the photon energy (eV) of the Hβ line, λ = 486.1 nm.
        Show answer

        $$\Delta E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \approx \frac{1240}{486.1} \approx \mathbf{2.55\ eV}$$

      2. An electron in a hydrogen atom drops from n = 5 to n = 2. Find the wavelength of the emitted photon.
        Show answer

        $$\Delta E = 13.6 \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{5^2}\right) = 13.6 \times 0.21 \approx 2.856\ \mathrm{eV}$$ $$\lambda \approx \frac{1240}{2.856} \approx \mathbf{434\ nm}$$ (the Hγ line, violet-blue).

      3. A photon has an energy of 2.9 eV. What colour is it, and which auroral species could emit it?
        Show answer

        $$\lambda \approx \frac{1240}{2.9} \approx \mathbf{428\ nm}$$ violet-blue, matching the 427.8 nm band of the N₂⁺ ion low in the atmosphere (about 90–110 km).

      4. Explain why the 630 nm red line of oxygen hardly appears at an altitude of 100 km.
        Show answer

        The ¹D state has to “wait” about 110 s before radiating. At 100 km the air is thousands of times denser than at 250 km, so during that time the atom almost certainly collides with N₂ or O₂ and loses its energy without emitting light (collisional quenching).

      5. An unknown salt gives a red flame. Through the spectroscope you see a broad red band at 640–690 nm and a blue line at 460.7 nm. Which metal is in the sample?
        Show answer

        Strontium. Lithium also gives a red flame, but only one narrow line at 670.8 nm and no blue line at 460.7 nm.

      6. The solar spectrum has a dark line at 589 nm. What does it tell us?
        Show answer

        It is an absorption line of sodium: cooler gas in the Sun's outer layers contains Na atoms that absorb exactly the 589 nm photons (the same position as sodium's D emission line).

      What is emission spectroscopy used for?

      One idea (every element emits and absorbs its own colours) is used every day, from observatories to laboratories and street lamps.

      ✦

      Astronomy

      For most celestial objects, light is almost the only “messenger” that brings information to Earth. Each dark line in a star's spectrum is the trace of an element in its outer gas; counting the lines and measuring their strength reveals what the star is made of and how hot it is.

      In 1868, while observing the solar corona during an eclipse, astronomers saw a yellow line at 587.6 nm that matched no known element. The new element was named helium (from the Greek helios, the Sun), and it was found on Earth only 27 years later.

      When a light source moves, its whole set of lines shifts slightly (the Doppler effect): towards red if it is receding, towards blue if it is approaching. Such tiny shifts led in 1995 to the discovery of the first planet orbiting a Sun-like star.

      Sources: Fraknoi et al. (2022); Mayor & Queloz (1995)

      ✺

      Fireworks

      Each firework colour is a giant flame test. Strontium salts give red, barium green, copper blue and sodium yellow; mixing strontium with copper produces purple.

      Interestingly, most of that light does not come from lone metal atoms but from small molecules formed in the flame, such as SrCl, SrOH, BaCl and CuCl. That is why firework makers often add a chlorine-rich ingredient to help these molecules form.

      Blue is the hardest colour: the CuCl molecule breaks apart if the flame is too hot, so a good blue firework has to burn at a “just right” temperature.

      Sources: Conkling & Mocella (2011); Steinhauser & Klapötke (2008)

      ⚗

      Chemical analysis

      In 1860, passing the light of a mineral-water flame through a spectroscope, Bunsen and Kirchhoff saw two unfamiliar blue lines. They belonged to an unknown element: caesium (Latin caesius, sky blue). The following year they discovered rubidium through its dark red lines.

      Today ICP-OES instruments use an argon plasma at about 6000–10000 K to excite a sample and measure the emission lines of dozens of elements at once. They can detect heavy metals at parts-per-billion levels, so they are used to check drinking water, soil and food.

      Sources: Kirchhoff & Bunsen (1860); Flowers et al. (2019)

      ◉

      Lighting

      Low-pressure sodium street lamps emit almost nothing but the yellow 589 nm doublet. They are among the most energy-efficient lamps, but under that single colour everything looks yellow or grey: you cannot tell a red car from a blue one.

      In a fluorescent lamp, mercury vapour emits most strongly at 253.7 nm, in the invisible ultraviolet. A phosphor coating inside the tube absorbs that UV and re-emits visible light in a few blue, green and red regions that together look almost white. Neon lamps use neon gas, which gives a characteristic red-orange glow.

      Sources: NIST ASD; Ling et al. (2016)

      About Aurora-LAB

      Learning science is more than memorising laws, concepts and results that others have discovered. Above all, it is the process of learning to observe the world, ask questions, test hypotheses and find answers for yourself, step by step.

      Aurora-LAB is built on that idea of education: it turns phenomena that are hard to observe, processes at atomic scales or across vast regions of space, into visual experiences that learners can actively explore.

      It is a virtual laboratory on atomic light and the aurora, developed by the STEM & Experiential Learning in Astronomy Research Group – USAC Astronomy Club. Through interactive simulations, learners can change the energy of an electron, explore how particles interact with the atmosphere, observe the light given off by heated elements and see how spectra are formed.

      Instead of only receiving a ready-made conclusion, you take part in the process that leads to it.

      From observation to understanding

      Aurora-LAB focuses on three closely connected topics, all opening up one of the most important ways humanity studies the universe: the study of light.

      1Photon emission by atoms

      What happens when an electron absorbs energy and moves to a higher level? Why, on returning to a lower level, does the atom emit photons of precise energies, creating characteristic spectral lines?

      2The aurora and the glowing atmosphere

      How do charged particles, energised by the solar wind, interact with atoms and molecules in the atmosphere? Why do altitude and atmospheric composition produce the different colours of the aurora?

      3From flame to spectrum

      What can the light of heated elements reveal about the composition of matter? How can a bright line in a spectrum become the signature that identifies an element?

      All three point to one basic principle: light carries information about the matter that emitted it or interacted with it. Every element has its own set of energy levels and its own spectral signature. By studying these signatures, scientists can determine the chemical composition, temperature and many physical properties of objects thousands or even millions of light-years away.

      From a small experiment with light in a classroom, learners can reach a research method that has opened the door to understanding stars, nebulae and distant galaxies.

      The educational philosophy behind Aurora-LAB

      We believe a meaningful science learning experience lies not only in how much knowledge learners receive, but also in the questions it raises and the ability to explore on their own that it builds.

      Aurora-LAB follows a few guiding principles:

      • 🔍
        Learning by exploring: Learners do not just watch results; they change the conditions of an experiment, follow what changes and work out the link between cause and effect.
      • 👁
        Making the invisible visible: Atomic processes, radiation the eye cannot see and events hundreds of kilometres up in the atmosphere are recreated in simulation, making abstract ideas more approachable.
      • 🔗
        Connecting theory and experiment: Physical concepts do not live apart in textbooks; they are placed in concrete situations to observe and test, so learners can link model, phenomenon and scientific explanation.
      • 📐
        Scientific accuracy as the foundation: Spectral data follow the NIST Atomic Spectra Database; auroral physics is checked against peer-reviewed work and material from the NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC). The content is written to be easy to follow while respecting the science (see ).
      • 🌏
        Widening access to science: Not every school has full spectroscopy equipment. By building a web-based simulation environment, Aurora-LAB hopes to lower barriers of equipment and conditions, giving school pupils, university students, teachers and astronomy enthusiasts more chances to explore.

      Science begins with curiosity

      Aurora-LAB does not aim to replace real experiments or the role of teachers. Instead, it hopes to be a supporting environment where learners can freely observe, experiment and form questions of their own.

      For understanding a phenomenon is not only knowing how it happens, but also why it happens and how we can test that explanation.

      Aurora-LAB is part of the educational ecosystem of the USAC Astronomy Club – University of Science, VNU-HCM, developed with the same aim: bringing scientific knowledge closer to the community while sparking a spirit of inquiry and scientific thinking in everyone.
